# Dialogue directions

## Scene purpose and scope

Define what this encounter needs to give the player: information, feeling, a relationship experience, a basis for a choice, or a combination. An introduction can establish a motive and practical contribution; ordinary company and grief call for different exchanges.

Keep the contracted participants, revelations, consequences, and entry conditions. When a scene has an optional speaker or suffix, make the base complete without it and let the addition respond to what is actually available in that branch.

Let the ending follow the fulfilled purpose. Acknowledgment, an unanswered question, or continued company can be enough. A change of conversational control or relationship is a possibility, not a required dramatic turn.

## Social action and conversational cause

At first contact, ordinarily give the speakers a social opening suited to their familiarity and situation. An urgent interruption or an ongoing conversation may begin elsewhere.

Give initiative to the person who wants something from the exchange. Let the other's response affect the next question, offer, refusal, or topic change. A connector can acknowledge the answer, close a subject, or make a transition; it need not add biography. Hesitation and silence can give someone time to choose what to say. Use them when the moment earns them, not as punctuation quotas.

Track what has just been asked, offered, accepted, or withdrawn, including deliberate evasions. Make sharp rebukes, confessions, praise, and changes of attitude proportionate to a cause the player can perceive in the exchange or established context. Private biography can guide a response, but an author's explanation after the scene cannot supply a missing cause.

## Voice, clarity, and subtext

Use the sheet as a range of responses shaped by this interlocutor and moment. A reserved speaker may answer a greeting briefly and explain a practical motive in connected sentences when asked. Brevity should preserve enough subject, cause, and consequence to understand the answer.

Choose the traits that matter here and let others remain implicit. Give subtext room through choices of attention, omissions, and responses rather than making speakers recite their psychological descriptions.

Tie practical contribution to believable experience and action in ordinary language. Preserve the game's limits when describing what someone can do; confidence or determination need not promise guaranteed success. Let vocabulary, sentence length, humor, profanity, and rhythm vary with character and circumstance instead of imposing one register on the cast.

## Knowledge from the player's position

Distinguish what each speaker knows, how they learned it, and what the player has actually been shown. When the source of knowledge matters, establish it economically: an introduction, a document, a shared task, or another supported connection can suffice.

Give enough context for the current stakes without explaining every name or relationship. An unfamiliar third party can remain unfamiliar if the statement about them is understandable. Add a relationship label only when supported or within the authorized creative latitude.

Keep inference separate from fact. Shared language may suggest solidarity without establishing a law about property or rewards. A metaphor, a sheet exercise, or a suggested line is not evidence that an event occurred. Read optional scenes without assuming the player saw other optional scenes; preserve uncertainty about routes, witnesses, and timing wherever the entry conditions leave them open.

## Invention and continuity

Create small connecting facts within the agreed latitude, preserving existing obstacles. Resolve routine details through judgment; consult the requester for contradictions with established facts or material plot, participant, revelation, or gameplay changes beyond that latitude.

Distinguish a draft proposal from an adopted continuity fact and from a source-derived fact. When a new fact is adopted for use, record its content, scope, and status in the existing authoritative document before subsequent authors rely on it: the GDD for world facts or shared rules, a character sheet for personal history or relationships, or the appropriate existing narrative document for other continuity. Retain its origin as an authored addition rather than attributing it to an older source. If the current role cannot edit that document, hand the fact to its authorized owner and keep the pending record explicit.

Persist only details that establish continuity. Social phrasing, incidental suggestions, metaphors, and hypothetical exercises do not automatically become canon. Use the existing documents rather than building a parallel archive of dialogue as authority.

## Reread and revise

Read as a player who has only the guaranteed prior context. Follow the spoken exchange in order: can the player understand the motive or stakes relevant here, identify the subject of each reply, and see why the next response follows? Check each eligible branch separately and then together. Check voice in context rather than counting verbal tics.

Judge repetition, greetings, connectors, and silence by their social or dramatic effect. Cut what serves no purpose; retain what makes the interaction intelligible.

Read the dialogue with only the staging the game can actually provide. Essential meaning must survive unavailable prop animation, unseen acting, or disabled effects. Keep production directions separate from spoken text and narration according to the medium's contract.

Revise material failures of comprehension, causality, voice, knowledge, or purpose. Give a concrete passage and reason for a requested change; stylistic alternatives alone do not require another round. Treat editorial judgment and technical validation as distinct from a human's response to the scene.
